Visual Thought Tokens: Reading Perception out of a Robot’s VLA, and Feeding It Back

Overview

Does a robot’s VLA already know where the cloth corner is, and just fail to use it? Vision-language-action models inherit rich perception from their pretrained vision-language backbone. My thesis asks whether that perception survives inside the model, whether a small decoder can read it back out, and whether handing it to the action head makes grasps better.

The model is X-VLA (0.9B, flow-matching action head) on a low-cost SO-ARM101 arm with a front camera and a wrist camera. All data is my own, recorded with LeRobot: 184 colour-cube pick-and-place demos, 70 “pick the cloth by a corner and drop it in the box” demos and 50 cloth-folding demos. Supervised by prof. Danijel Skočaj and dr. Domen Tabernik at the ViCoS lab.

A decoder that reads the frozen backbone

Frozen X-VLA tokens→Visual-thought decoder→Expert’s view

The visual-thought decoder is a bank of learned query tokens that cross-attend to X-VLA’s internal token sequence, the fused image-and-instruction tokens the action head already consumes. Its output, the visual-thought tokens, is trained to match an external expert while X-VLA itself stays frozen. The same template gets three different jobs:

  • DINOv2 features: reproduce the patch tokens of a DINOv2 ViT-B/14, a strong general-purpose vision model.
  • Cloth-corner maps: reconstruct the direction fields of CeDiRNet, a ViCoS network whose maps point toward graspable cloth corners.
  • Token grounding: point at which of X-VLA’s own image tokens matter for the instruction right now.

What the backbone already knows

Both experts can be recovered from the frozen X-VLA tokens. Projected to colour with PCA, the decoded DINOv2 tokens (right) organise the scene like the real DINOv2 features (middle): the arm, the table, the cloth and the cube each keep their own colour. A one-layer decoder of about 5M parameters rebuilds the CeDiRNet direction maps with a validation error of 0.015, enough to point at the cloth corners.

Token grounding: which tokens matter right now?

X-VLA sees each frame as a 7×7 grid of image tokens. For grounding, the decoder produces a single visual-thought token that acts as a query and scores every cell of that grid, so the answer lives in the same token space the policy acts from. The best few cells are then refined to a 14×14 grid using finer features from X-VLA’s own vision tower. The idea is inspired by MolmoPoint’s pointing tokens.

  • Data: 4,069 image–instruction pairs over 2,191 frames, including 1,524 negatives where the named cube isn’t there.
  • Precision first: a wrong highlight would steer the robot to the wrong object, so models are selected on a precision-weighted F-score (F0.25). The best checkpoint reaches 88% precision at 60% recall on held-out frames.
  • It follows the task, not just the word: before the grasp the named cube lights up, from both cameras. Once the cube is in the gripper, relevance moves to the box.

Feeding it back to the policy

  • Implicit guidance: fine-tune the whole policy with the decoder attached, adding the reconstruction loss to the action loss. The policy’s inputs don’t change; the extra loss only keeps the corner structure recoverable inside the backbone.
  • Explicit guidance: pass the decoder’s 32×32 cloth-corner tokens straight to the action transformer, either appended to its input for all 24 layers or inserted only at layers 6, 12 and 18, each with an optional learned gate.
  • Fair comparison: every guided model is compared with a control trained for the same extra steps without guidance, so gains can’t come from training longer.

Results on the real robot

Thesis ↗

16 trials per model on each cloth task (four cloth layouts × 4) and 48 on the cube task.

  • Picking the right corner: on cloth-to-box, the policy picked the correct corner 50% of the time with extra training alone, 75% with implicit guidance and 87.5% with guidance at layers 6/12/18. The original baseline managed 25%.
  • Grasping it: implicit guidance grasped best once it had found the corner (67% grasp score vs. 39% for layer insertion). Finding the target and grasping it are separate skills.
  • How it enters matters: appending the same tokens to the input collapsed the policy to 12.5% correct corners. Inserting them at a few layers worked.
  • Folding: implicit and layer-inserted guidance found the corner 16/16 times, but fold quality stayed low for every model (19–34%).
  • Cubes: with DINOv2 guidance, picking the cube of the named colour rose from 83% to 90%. The baseline’s failures were hesitations, stopping midway between two cubes; with guidance it committed, though occasionally to the wrong cube (5 of 48).

What I took away

  • The backbone holds more task-relevant perception than the action head uses, and a few million parameters are enough to read it out.
  • Guidance reliably helps the policy choose the right target. Turning that into a precise grasp is a separate problem, so I report grasp accuracy given a correct target alongside the usual success rates.
  • Getting X-VLA to work on the SO-ARM101 took real effort: switching LeRobot to end-effector actions, normalising the robot state, training on varied lighting and smoothing commands to reduce shaking. Small camera shifts still hurt it.
